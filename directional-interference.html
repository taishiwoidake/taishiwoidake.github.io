This exploratory line asks whether directional reversal-interference signals can distinguish spatial interaction kernels that are indistinguishable after directional averaging, within a specified physical model.

RESEARCH NOTES

An inverse problem in spatial response

A response measured after a controlled perturbation may depend on the direction in which a system is displaced or probed. If distinct interaction models yield the same averaged signal, direction-resolved measurements can provide additional constraints.

The central question is whether those constraints are sufficient to identify a parameter or distinguish a specified family of kernels. Identifiability depends on the measurement model, not just on the presence of anisotropy.

Two objects can have the same average width while looking very different when examined from different directions.

INTUITIVE PICTURE

The simple picture

A long thin object and a broad round object can share an average measurement even though their directional dimensions differ.

SCIENTIFIC QUESTION

The inverse problem

Prepare and control the geometry of an interferometric protocol; compare measurements under different orientations and test whether the source kernel is identifiable.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT CLAIM · A directional pattern is not, on its own, evidence of a new gravitational force.
RESEARCH NOTES

Why averaging loses information

For a twice differentiable scalar potential V in three dimensions, its local second directional derivative along a unit vector u is uᵀH_Vu, where H_V is its Hessian. Averaging uniformly over directions gives Tr(H_V)/3 because the average of uuᵀ is I/3.

Distinct Hessians with the same trace therefore have the same directional average. The average retains the isotropic component and discards the trace-free component. This is a standard mathematical identity, not a new physical law.

RESEARCH NOTES

Radial and transverse curvature

For a radial potential V(r), at r>0 the Hessian has eigenvalues V″(r) along the radial direction and V′(r)/r in each of the two transverse directions. For V(r)=1/r these are 2/r³, −1/r³ and −1/r³.

The trace vanishes away from the origin even though the radial and transverse responses differ. Hence the average cannot distinguish this curvature from another traceless curvature using that single statistic alone. Recovering the potential would require more than one local measurement and appropriate boundary assumptions.

✳   4 / DIRECTIONAL INFERENCE   ✳

How would
we measure it?

The research question is to derive a physically measurable contrast from a controlled forward-and-reverse sequence, then determine whether its directional dependence constrains the underlying kernel.

CONTROL

Define two paths

Specify the geometry, separation, coupling, state preparation, forward and reverse dynamics. Hold nuisance parameters fixed when comparing orientations.

OBSERVABLE

Measure visibility

Use a precisely defined visibility observable, and propagate its measurement uncertainty. Any logarithmic visibility contrast requires positive values and a justified model relating it to the kernel.

IDENTIFIABILITY

Compare explanations

An inverse problem requires more than different predictions at one parameter value. Test identifiability against parameter degeneracies, measurement error and alternative models.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT CLAIM · No experiment has here been shown to reconstruct a spatial kernel or discover a new physical law.
RESEARCH NOTES

Connecting curvature to a measurable signal

A proposed interference protocol must derive its actual observable from the preparation, dynamical generator, control operations and readout. A Hessian expression is useful only if the connection to a measurable contrast is justified under stated approximations.

One can then ask which directions and distances make two candidate models distinguishable and whether the predictions remain separated after including nuisance parameters and noise. This is the inverse-problem stage of the research.

RESEARCH NOTES

Limits of a directional reconstruction

A finite set of measurements can constrain a finite-dimensional parameter family when the forward map is identifiable. It need not determine an arbitrary unknown spatial function. Ambiguities and stability against noise must be assessed separately from uniqueness in an ideal model.

This page presents background and research questions. The interpretation of an actual reversal-interference signal requires a validated physical model and independent evidence.
